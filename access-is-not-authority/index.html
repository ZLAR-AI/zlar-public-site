Access Is Not Authority

Trust at the point of execution is the fast path to AI adoption

Intelligence is probabilistic. Authorization for protected actions must be deterministic.

Technology moves at the speed of innovation. Adoption moves at the speed of trust.

Minister Evan Solomon's observation identifies the challenge precisely.

Organizations want the benefits of AI. They slow adoption when they cannot trust the execution boundary: the point where an AI-generated intention becomes a real consequence.

That trust can be engineered.

What the incident reveals

OpenAI's disclosure says its evaluation deliberately tasked an agent system powered by a combination of models with pursuing advanced exploitation.

The system found a path to the open internet. It obtained credentials, chained vulnerabilities, moved laterally, and reached Hugging Face production infrastructure.

The evaluation task and those consequential crossings were separate things.

Tasking describes what a system has been asked to pursue. Authority determines which consequences the surrounding environment will accept.

Open internet access was one crossing. Credential use was another. Lateral movement was another. Entry into production infrastructure was another. Each destination represented an opportunity to ask:

Does this exact action carry recognized authority to occur here?

The protected-action primitive

A protected action is an action an organization determines must carry explicit authority before it becomes real — transferring funds, disclosing sensitive information, changing access rights, deploying software, connecting to an external network, operating critical infrastructure, issuing a consequential public or legal communication.

The governing primitive:

A protected action may execute only when the destination verifies explicit, recognized authority bound to that exact action.

The destination is the system being asked to make the consequence real. It owns the final boundary.

Access is not authority

An AI system may obtain network connectivity, a valid identity, an authenticated session, API access, credentials, administrative capability, or a position inside a trusted environment. These establish identity, access, or capability. They should never, by themselves, provide sufficient authority for a designated protected action.

Authentication answers who or what is making a request. Authorization answers whether this exact action may occur, for this purpose, within this scope, at this destination, at this time.

A system can be exactly who it claims to be and still lack authority for the action it is attempting.

Trust must belong to the environment

A protected system should not depend on an AI system correctly interpreting the limits of its own authority. AI can propose, explain, monitor, detect, and investigate. The final enforcement boundary requires deterministic verification.

The destination does not need to infer intent, score behaviour, or ask one AI system whether another should be trusted. It verifies whether recognized authorization exists for the exact protected action. If authorization is valid, the action proceeds. If accountable human authority is required, the action pauses. If authorization is missing, expired, replayed, or mismatched, the destination refuses execution and leaves a receipt.

Trust becomes a property of the environment, rather than a prediction about model behaviour.

Why this is the fast path

Organizations slow AI adoption when every consequential action carries the same uncertainty. Deterministic authorization creates separation: routine actions within pre-authorized policy proceed automatically; human authority enters where policy requires it; unauthorized actions are refused at the destination; every decision and outcome leaves attributable evidence.

Human involvement becomes precise rather than universal. More routine work can be automated because the protected boundary remains enforceable. Trust becomes infrastructure. Infrastructure accelerates adoption.

A control principle Canada can lead

Public and critical systems shall execute designated protected actions only after the receiving destination deterministically verifies recognized authorization bound to the exact action, actor, purpose, scope, destination, and time.

This requirement shall apply regardless of whether the request originates from an AI system, application, workflow engine, human interface, administrative tool, or compromised credential.

Missing, invalid, expired, replayed, wrong-destination, or otherwise mismatched authorization shall result in refusal and a durable receipt.

This principle does not depend on a particular model, vendor, or interface. It governs how consequential action becomes real.

The mechanism

For each protected action: the requested action is converted into an exact, canonical form; deterministic policy produces ALLOW, DENY, or ASK; ALLOW, or accountable human approval following ASK, produces signed authorization bound to that exact action; DENY, silence, and expiry produce non-authorizing receipts; the destination verifies the authorization before execution; valid authorization is consumed with execution so it cannot be replayed; execution or refusal produces a destination-signed receipt.

The destination verifies receipt type, recognized issuer and signature, exact action, actor or principal, purpose and scope, destination, applicable policy, freshness and expiry, required state conditions, nonce and replay status, and accountable human decision when required.

No recognized authorization means no execution.

Every path must reach the same door

The control must apply regardless of how the request arrives. An AI agent, application, workflow engine, administrative interface, human operator, or compromised credential should face the same protected boundary. Every claimed execution path must converge on the destination-owned guard.

An unguarded path is an ungoverned path.

A control surface for standards, audit, forensics and insurance

A statute binds the labs already inclined to comply. What actually drove security improvement across the software industry wasn't legislation first — it was insurance underwriting, audit standards, and vendor accountability. Insurers and auditors cannot price or certify a risk they cannot verify, and there is no forensic standard yet for establishing, after an agentic incident, exactly what was authorized, by whom, and when.

Deterministic, destination-verified authorization creates exactly that surface, as a byproduct of normal operation, not as an afterthought:

  • Which actions are designated as protected?
  • Does every execution path reach the destination-owned guard?
  • What recognized authority must accompany each action?
  • Are missing, expired, replayed, and mismatched authorizations refused?
  • Can an execution be traced to the exact authorization that permitted it?
  • Does every refusal and execution leave durable evidence?
  • Can an independent evaluator reproduce the result?

Standards bodies can define interoperable requirements against these questions. Auditors can test enforcement and side-door coverage. Forensic investigators can reconstruct which action was requested, which authority was presented, and what the destination did. Insurers can evaluate observable controls and attributable outcomes, rather than relying entirely on predictions about model behaviour.

This is where architecture, standards, forensics, and underwriting reinforce one another — not because this was designed to solve insurance markets, but because the private forcing functions those markets depend on need an attributable, verifiable record to act on. That record either exists by design, or it doesn't exist at all.

How this complements existing controls

Containment, model evaluation, monitoring, laboratory controls, and emergency shutdown capabilities remain essential. They reduce the probability that dangerous behaviour reaches a protected system, and provide ways to respond when it does.

Destination-verified authorization answers the next question: what must be true before the protected system agrees to execute the action? This boundary remains meaningful when containment fails, credentials are compromised, or an unexpected route is discovered.

The complete reference architecture

The architecture does not require intelligence to become deterministic. It makes protected consequences deterministically governable.

A practical Canadian path

Canada can advance this principle through coordinated action and sustained examination: designate protected-action classes for public and critical systems; develop standards for destination-owned authorization verification; apply the requirement regardless of request origin or route; require exact action binding, expiration, and replay protection; require accountable human authority where policy calls for it; require execution and refusal receipts; establish forensic standards for attributable agentic incidents; give auditors and insurers a common control surface to evaluate; test whether every execution path converges on the same protected boundary.

This work belongs across policymakers, deep domain experts, standards bodies, innovators, auditors, incident responders, and insurers. It is specific enough to act on and important enough to examine continuously.

Final thought

Intelligence will continue to become more capable. It will find routes that designers did not anticipate.

The fast path to adoption is an environment that can use that intelligence while retaining deterministic control over protected consequences.

Intelligence may find another path.

Every successful path must still meet the same guarded door.
